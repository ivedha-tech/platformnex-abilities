# Ideation Frameworks

Pick the lens that fits the idea. The aim is to unlock thinking, not to complete a checklist.

## SCAMPER

Transform an existing idea with seven operations.

- **Substitute:** swap a component, technology, audience, or business model.
- **Combine:** merge with another product, service, or idea that does not usually go with it.
- **Adapt:** borrow from another industry, era, or natural system.
- **Modify:** make one feature 10x bigger, or strip everything to the minimum.
- **Put to other uses:** a different user, problem, or context.
- **Eliminate:** drop a feature, the configuration, or half the steps.
- **Reverse:** change the order of steps, or who does the work (user vs system).

Best for improving existing products; weaker for greenfield ideas.

## How Might We (HMW)

Start from an observed pain and phrase it as "How might we [outcome] for [specific user] without [constraint]?" Write several framings of the same problem, since each unlocks different solutions.

- Good: narrow enough to act on, broad enough to allow creativity, contains a real tension.
- Bad: too broad ("make users happy"), too narrow ("add a settings button"), or with the solution baked in ("build a chatbot").

Best for pulling someone off a premature solution and back to the problem.

## First principles

1. What do we know is true, as opposed to assumed or conventional?
2. List every assumption, including the obvious ones.
3. For each, ask whether it is a hard limit or just habit.
4. Rebuild from the truths alone.

Best when every idea looks like a small tweak of the status quo.

## Jobs to Be Done

Focus on what the user is trying to accomplish, not on what they say they want.

- Functional job: the task to complete.
- Emotional job: how they want to feel.
- Social job: how they want to be seen.

Template: "When I [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]." Competitors are whatever else gets the job done, which may be a different category entirely.

Best when you are unsure you are solving the right problem.

## Constraint-based ideation

Impose a limit on purpose: one day to build, one feature only, no obvious technology, free forever, a user who has never used a computer, a billion users versus ten.

Best for cutting an idea that is growing too large or too fuzzy.

## Pre-mortem

1. Assume it shipped a year ago and failed. Why?
2. List every plausible cause: technical, market, team, timing.
3. For each: preventable, or a sign the idea must change?
4. Decide which failures you accept and which are fatal.

Best in phase 2, for ideas that feel good but are untested.

## Analogous inspiration

Ask which field has already solved a similar problem, how a well-known product would approach it, what natural or historical precedent exists. Match on structure, not surface: "a two-sided marketplace solving trust between strangers" is structural; "X for Y" is surface.

Best in phase 1, to generate variations that differ from the obvious one.
